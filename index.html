01
Full identity system

NOIR — Premium Cold Brew Coffee Brand

Problem. Cold brew branding tends to default to one of two modes: sterile minimalism or try-hard "cool café" energy. Neither earns trust with someone who wants their coffee to feel considered, not decorated. Audience: urban professionals, 22–32, people who read a room before they walk into it.

Concept. NOIR is built around a single idea — identity is something you carry into a space, not something a brand hands you. The mark's central "i" is rendered as a figure standing in a doorway: a hat, a single eye in copper, a body reduced to one upright stroke. It's the one letter that doesn't match its neighbors, deliberately — identity as the thing that stands apart, even inside a system built to look unified.

Brand identity · Coffee
000000 — hat, accent only 5C3D2E — primary block color B5754A — copper, accent only F0E6D6 — cream, negative space

Execution

A three-color system (black, brown, cream, with copper as a single accent — never a fill) carries across cup, card, and poster without needing to be reinvented per format. Montserrat was chosen for the same reason the palette stays to three colors: restraint that still has range, reading clean at a glance but carrying enough weight variation to hold tone shifts across contexts.

Why it holds up

Trend-driven identity work ages fast. NOIR is built to last because it isn't chasing a current aesthetic — it's built on a piece of reasoning (a figure, a doorway, an odd letter out) that doesn't expire when the trend does. The same wordmark that anchors a still, moody poster also holds up at cup-sleeve scale and inside a fast-cut Reel storyboard.

02
Research-to-visual translation

How Dating Apps Rewire You — Instagram Carousel

The brief. Fifteen-plus strong research angles on dating app psychology — the engagement loop, paradox of choice, thin-slicing, hypergamy and selection dynamics, opportunity cost, and more — individually solid, but stacked together they competed for attention rather than building one argument.

The process. Scribble first, structure second, software last. Fifteen candidate ideas were cut down to the three that actually carry the piece — The Reduction (how a person becomes four categories on a form), The Loop (the engagement mechanism that keeps you searching), and How Connection Actually Forms (the real counter-mechanism: shared experience over curated profiles). The full structure was sketched by hand, cover to close, before any software was touched, then rebuilt as a 5-slide carousel in Canva — choosing illustration sets by hand and iterating the cover and icon clusters across multiple passes for a consistent visual language rather than a stock-photo patchwork.

Content design · Instagram carousel
"Your goal is to leave. The loop's goal is to keep you in it."

Role of AI — used for

Pressure-testing the argument, not generating it: narrowing fifteen research angles to the three that belonged together, catching redundancy where two separate loop diagrams were saying the same thing twice, and reviewing each Canva export for icon-style consistency, hierarchy, and clutter round by round.

Role of AI — not used for

Not used to design the layout, choose the illustrations, or build the file. Every slide was assembled by hand in Canva, image by image, with each visual decision — which icon set, where the arrows sit, what gets cut — made and re-made across several review passes, not generated in one pass.

How a project runs

Process
1

Intake — read the brief before opening the canvas

Every project starts with the actual constraint: who the audience is, what's already been tried, what "done wrong" looks like for this brand specifically. For research-heavy content (like the dating apps carousel), this stage includes gathering and sorting far more raw material than will ever ship — fifteen angles down to three, not three from the start.

2

Sketch — paper before software

Concepts get sketched by hand first, structure and all, before Illustrator or Canva opens. This forces the idea to hold up on its own — a mark or a slide structure that only works because of a software effect isn't actually working.

3

Build — hand-built, not auto-generated

Vector work is built anchor point by anchor point, never auto-traced. Content layouts (carousels, one-pagers) are assembled image by image, with each visual decision — icon set, arrow placement, what gets cut — made deliberately rather than templated.

4

Pressure-test — AI as critic, not creator

AI tools are used the same way across every project: to catch redundancy, flag inconsistency, and stress-test whether an idea actually holds — never to generate the design, layout, or copy itself. That line stays fixed regardless of project type.

5

Deliver — built for every context it'll actually appear in

A mark gets checked at cup-sleeve scale and poster scale. A carousel gets checked slide-by-slide for pacing, not just as individual images. The deliverable is judged by whether it holds up outside the file it was built in.

Where AI fits, precisely: across every project on this page, AI is used to pressure-test an argument or a design decision — narrowing options, catching redundancy, reviewing consistency round by round. It is never used to design the layout, choose the visuals, or generate the final file. That distinction is deliberate and doesn't change project to project.
